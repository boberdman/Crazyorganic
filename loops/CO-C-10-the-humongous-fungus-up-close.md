## Stage 1 — Topic Brief (Research)

**Pillar:** 1 — The unexplored kingdom (wonder)
**Working title:** The Humongous Fungus, Up Close *(kept as written in the backlog — no de-hyping needed. "Humongous fungus" isn't a contested thesis in the brand's voice; it's the documented real nickname, coined by Michigan journalists in 1992 and used by the US Forest Service itself. "Up close" is the load-bearing word: it signals the deliberate angle shift away from Series A's CO-A-05 "The Largest Living Thing Is a Fungus," which asked* that *and used the size to break the definition of an individual. This piece asks* how *— the mechanism of becoming the largest, and the detective work of proving it — so it extends rather than repeats A5, exactly as the backlog line intends ("how one organism becomes the largest on Earth… extends the staged Largest Living Thing").)*
**The big question / hook:** Not *that* a fungus is the biggest living thing (we've said that), but *how* a lifeform with no skeleton, no center, and no plan becomes the largest organism ever measured — and how a handful of scientists ever proved that mushrooms popping up half a mile apart are one individual you can't see.

**Key points:**
1. The mushroom is the fruit, not the organism. The honey mushrooms at the bases of dying firs each fall are seasonal fruiting tips of a single underground mycelial network; the "largest organism" is the network, not a giant mushroom. Growing underground is *why* it can beat any above-ground size ceiling. [established]
2. The growth engine: **rhizomorphs** — black, root-like cords ("bootlaces"/"shoestrings") of bundled parallel hyphae, with a melanized protective wall, a conductive core (water, nutrients, even air), and an apically dominant growing tip. They let the fungus *travel* — grow through open soil from a food base toward the next living tree, which loose mycelium can't do. Malheur spread rate ≈ 0.7–3.3 ft/year. [established]
3. How it was proven to be one individual (the "up close" detective beat): you can't dig up a 3.7-sq-mi invisible web, so scientists let the fungus fingerprint itself — **somatic-incompatibility pairing** (same genet fuses; strangers throw a dark rejection line) plus **DNA genotyping**. Matching genotype across the ground = one organism. This is what grew the 1988 estimate from ~400 acres to 2,385 acres. [established]
4. The record-holder specifics: *Armillaria ostoyae* (syn. *A. solidipes*), Malheur National Forest (Blue/Strawberry Mountains, ~11 mi E of Prairie City), **2,385 acres (~3.7 sq mi)**, and it's **one of five** genetically distinct *A. ostoyae* individuals in that same forest patch (50–2,385 acres each). [established]
5. It's a killer, and the size and the menace are the same fact: *Armillaria* root disease. Rhizomorph reaches a root → penetrates → white mycelial fan girdles the tree under the bark → conifer dies over ~20–50 years → fungus keeps eating the dead wood and pushes on. Disease centers = widening canopy openings. The record body is a map of everything it has eaten. [established]
6. Genome postscript: the Hungarian *Armillaria* genome work (Sipos, Nagy et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 2017) — genome expansion with genes specific to rhizomorph development, possibly repurposed from mushroom-stalk genes. [established (published finding), stated modestly]
7. Honesty beat (the "Where we get honest" box): the famous **35,000 tons / 8,650 years** are estimates, not measurements — mass extrapolated from area × density, age back-calculated from area ÷ spread rate, and the age ranges widely (~2,400–8,650 yr) by method. "Largest" also depends on defining a clonal network as one individual and on ranking by area vs. mass (seagrass off Australia and the Pando aspen grove are rivals). Plus a genuine open puzzle: the 2018 re-study of the *original* Michigan "humongous fungus" (*A. gallica*, 245 samples) found a strikingly low mutation rate — how it grows so big/old while changing so little is unresolved. [size/age = established-as-estimate; mutation-rate puzzle = preliminary]

**Get-it-right traps handled:**
- **Species:** the Oregon record-holder is *Armillaria ostoyae*; the *original* 1992 Michigan "humongous fungus" is *Armillaria gallica* (originally called *A. bulbosa*) — a *different* species. Wikipedia's "Armillaria ostoyae" page conflates them (it calls the 2,500-yr Michigan *gallica* individual "*A. ostoyae*"). The Oregon Encyclopedia and the Smithsonian/Anderson reporting are explicit and correct; the piece follows them and states the distinction in-text ("a related honey fungus, *Armillaria gallica*, not the Oregon species").
- **Mushroom ≠ organism** (beat #1) and **estimate ≠ measurement** (beat #7) are the two other get-it-right moves.

**Claims-risk flag:** none for §4 hard lines — no psilocybin/cultivation/sourcing, no health/medical/therapeutic claim, no legal/financial. Honey-mushroom edibility (mentioned by the Oregon Encyclopedia, with a toxic-lookalike caveat) was **deliberately cut at Content stage** rather than hedged — no foraging/edibility content, same discipline as CO-C-07/CO-C-08.
